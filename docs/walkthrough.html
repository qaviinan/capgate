Capped Escalation Gateway

A company runs a small, cheap model for most questions and pays a large model for the hard ones, under a fixed spending cap. This project builds the layer in front of both that decides, request by request, which answer to return, and never spends past the cap, even when parts of it crash.

The problems

Each of these is documented in production systems today. The sources are in the checkpoint's reference list.

Shared caps get overspent

Gateways run as several copies. Each copy counts spending after the bill arrives, so requests in flight at the same moment push the total past the cap.

Confidence scores can be wrong

A score says how likely the cheap answer is right. It can be biased, stuck at one value, or pushed around by crafted inputs. We receive it from another system and cannot fully trust it.

The cheap model degrades silently

A wrong prompt template or a wrong model version makes answers worse without any error. Nothing crashes, so nothing alerts.

Escalating costs time too

An escalated request waits for the cheap model, then the score, then the expensive model. A burst of escalations also queues up at the expensive provider.

The distributed systems question. Several gateway copies each decide on their own, with no shared counter in the request path. How do they together respect one spending cap, one error budget and one latency target, through crashes, network cut-offs, and copies being added or removed?

One request, end to end

  1. The request reaches a replica

    Ray Serve picks one of the gateway replicas. No replica talks to another.

  2. The cheap tier answers

    Every request gets a cheap answer first. The scorer attaches a confidence score s: its guess at the chance this answer is right.

  3. The replica estimates what escalating would cost

    Two numbers. A cost bound: the most the expensive answer could cost, from a length predictor tuned so real cost rarely exceeds it. And an extra delay, from how busy the expensive tier is right now.

  4. Keep or escalate

    escalate if  (1 − s) > money price × cost bound + latency price × extra delay
    and the lease still holds at least the cost bound

    The left side is the expected gain: the chance the cheap answer is wrong. The right side is what escalating costs, in the replica's own prices. If the replica has no valid lease, it returns the cheap answer with a flag. It never spends money it does not hold.

  5. If escalating: reserve, call, settle

    The replica sets the cost bound aside from its lease, then calls the expensive tier with a token limit so the bill cannot exceed the bound. It returns that answer, charges the real cost, and frees the rest.

  6. Sometimes: audit in the background

    For a small share of cheap answers, the replica also asks the expensive tier after replying. It compares the two answers and sends the result to the monitor. The client is not kept waiting. Audits are paid from the same lease.

  7. Adjust the prices

    The money price rises when the lease is being spent faster than its remaining time allows, and falls when slower. The latency price rises when slow responses exceed the target. This is how a replica becomes pickier when money or time is tight.

Sharing one budget without a shared counter

Leases

The allocator gives each replica a slice of the cap that is valid for a short time, about 30 seconds. A replica spends only from its own slice, with no network call per request. Before the slice expires it asks for a new one and hands back what it did not use.

Why the cap cannot be exceeded

Budget counts as gone the moment it is granted. It comes back only when the replica holding it reports what it really spent. A replica that crashes never reports, so its slice is lost, never handed out twice. Total spent is always at most total granted, which is at most the cap.

Epochs

Every allocator has a number that goes up each time a new one takes over. Leases carry that number. Replicas refuse anything from an older allocator, so a stale one cannot hand out budget the new one already counted.

The price paid

Safety costs some unused budget: slices stranded by crashes, and the gap between each cost bound and the real cost. Measuring that waste against lease length is one of the results.

Future work

  • Self-hosted expensive tier. If the large model runs on our own GPUs, the cap becomes capacity. Leases would grant a request rate, not dollars.
  • Allocator on Raft. Three or five allocator copies agreeing on the grant log, in place of one primary and one backup.
  • Hold answers until checked. For high-stakes requests, do not release a cheap answer until its audit agrees.
  • Per-customer bounds. A separate cost bound and cap for each customer of the gateway.

Known limits we will state: results come from replayed answers, not live traffic. Audits use up budget, which hurts most under a tight cap. Pacing for a single router is already published, so our claims rest on the distributed parts.
